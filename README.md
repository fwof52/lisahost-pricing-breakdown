## How the billing discounts change the real price

Quarterly billing takes 10% off, annual 20%, two-year 30% — and renewals are priced like the first order, so the discount isn't a first-term bait you lose at renewal. Here's the math on the ¥88/month 9929 Basic card:

- Billed monthly for a year: ¥1,056
- Billed annually: ¥845, which works out to ¥70.4/month and saves ¥211
- Billed two years upfront: ¥1,478, or ¥61.6/month, saving ¥634
- Billed quarterly: ¥238 against ¥264 monthly-billed, saving ¥26

The arithmetic is honest across the catalog — the percentages are the same on every line, and they apply again at renewal. If you already know you'll keep the plan past a few months, paying annually is effectively free money.

## Why the same "residential" label covers ¥199/year and ¥169/month

Because the storefront uses one phrase — 双ISP家宽住宅, dual-ISP home broadband — for at least three different products, and only one of them is a home connection in the physical sense. This is the single most important thing to understand before comparing prices.

1. **¥169/month — real-house residential VDS.** The vendor states the hardware sits in an actual US house on a carrier fibre line, and the card names the carrier: Atlas Networks, Astound Broadband, or T-Mobile/Frontier. This is the only line that's a home connection physically.
2. **¥68/month — dual-ISP "residential".** A datacenter VPS whose IP database record names an ISP. Platforms and fraud checkers will often read it as residential; it isn't one in the physical sense.
3. **¥199/year — native IP.** A plain native address with no residential claim at all. The storefront's "from ¥16/month" badge points at this product.

The practical rule: pay VDS money only if your use case genuinely needs the physical-home property. For most account work, the ¥68 card's database record does the job — and whatever grade you buy, score the delivered address inside the 24-hour replacement window before you rely on it.

## The fine print that changes what you'll actually pay

Three rules sit in the terms rather than on the plan cards, and each one has real cost implications.

**The refund isn't unconditional.** Marketing pages say 48 hours; the terms add a cap — if you've used more than 5% of your traffic or 20 GB, whichever is smaller, the refund is void. Setup and payment-processing fees are excluded, and past 48 hours you waive the right entirely. Two lines override even that: refunds on the home-broadband VDS go to account balance only, and the shared-NAT line doesn't refund at all. If there's any chance you'll return a plan, keep traffic near zero for the first two days.

**There's no uptime promise.** The terms state, verbatim, "We do not offer any service level guarantee" — no uptime figure, no credit table anywhere — and the document hasn't been updated since 25 January 2021. Budget for an outage as a cost of doing business rather than a breach you can claim on.

**IP quality still needs testing.** The vendor advertises a Scamalytics score of 0 on its dual-ISP lines and replaces a blacklisted IP free within 24 hours of provisioning. Against that, two independent first-hand reports measured IPQS fraud scores of 99 and 100 on delivered "residential" IPs — one of them on the real-house VDS line — and in one case support refused a replacement, calling ipinfo.io the authority. Run the delivered address through a checker the day it arrives; that's the whole game.
